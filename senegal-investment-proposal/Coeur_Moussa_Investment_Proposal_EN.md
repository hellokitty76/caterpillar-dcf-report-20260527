## 1. Executive Summary

Coeur-Moussa is a **~60-hectare new town center located approximately 45 km from Dakar**, delivering **2,000 social and affordable rental homes** for members of the **Chantifa cooperative**. It is developed by **SIVEP** with financial partner **Horizon Capital & Advisory** and designed by the architecture firm **Kama & Associés**. The program is a complete new town — not just housing — comprising schools, nurseries and educational institutions, a medical center, commercial and administrative buildings, sports/cultural/leisure facilities, places of worship (a church and a mosque), landscaped public space, and a fully graded road network with bike lanes and green medians.

**The opportunity for a Chinese partner is twofold.** Financially, the 2,000 villas are held as a single-owner **rental portfolio**, producing recurring rental yield rather than one-off sales proceeds. Strategically — and this is the heart of the proposition — the project is a **beachhead into the West African market for Chinese building materials, equipment, and companies.** Building 2,000 homes plus a full town's worth of infrastructure is a large, repeatable order for Chinese-exported steel, aluminum, tiles, sanitaryware, and electrical equipment — and for solar panels. The Chinese side participates as an **equity investor**, with discussions running through the **Shanghai Chamber of Commerce**.

This dual return — rental income plus trade/market access — is reinforced by strong tailwinds: the project matches the "low-cost social housing" interest Chinese participants raised at the June 2025 China-Senegal economic forum in Hangzhou; it aligns squarely with Senegal's national housing strategy; and because the base design contains **no solar or renewable element**, a Chinese-supplied photovoltaic component is a clean, additive layer (Section 9), not a retrofit. Chinese export-credit tools (Sinosure, China Eximbank) exist precisely to support this kind of export-led investment.

## 3. Project Overview

**Location & scale.** Coeur-Moussa sits approximately 45 km from Dakar — the same greater-Dakar growth corridor as Diamniadio (~35 km) and Senegal's Scaling Solar plants — on a master-planned site of roughly **60 hectares**. The program comprises **2,000 individual villas**, each on its own plot, in two types:

| Type | Land plot | Built area | Layout |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Milvilla F3** | 150 m² | ~50.65 m² | Living room 15 m², master bedroom 12 m², bedroom 10 m², kitchen 6 m², toilet 4 m², family space 3.65 m² |
| **Milvilla F4** | 200 m² | ~73.41 m² | Living room 15.48 m², master bedroom 14.36 m², bathroom 3.41 m², two bedrooms at 10 m², kitchen 6 m², toilet 4 m², family space 13.16 m² |

**A complete new town.** Beyond the dwellings, the master plan includes an administrative building, commercial spaces, schools, nurseries and educational institutions, a medical center, sports/cultural/leisure facilities, a church and a mosque, sustainable waste management, resident security, and landscaped public space (shade areas, walkways, children's playgrounds, public gardens, and parking). The road network is fully graded — a 30 m primary arterial, 20 m secondary, 15 m tertiary, 12 m paved internal block roads, and 5 m pedestrian paths — with bike lanes, wide sidewalks, and central green medians on the main axes, reflecting a deliberate emphasis on soft mobility and quality of life.

**Design and construction.** The architecture is simple and harmonious, drawing on Coeur-Moussa's local character, with strong natural ventilation for year-round comfort. Construction follows a conventional reinforced-concrete frame (BAEL 91), hollow-block masonry, hollow-core (hourdis creux) floor slabs, monocouche roof waterproofing, ceramic and faience finishes, anti-termite-treated hardwood joinery, anodized aluminum windows and doors, PVC plumbing with on-site septic tanks, and NFC 15-100-compliant electrical installations. The base design provides for a **standard grid connection only** — no solar element — with mandatory third-party technical control (Bureau de Contrôle) and decennial insurance throughout construction.

**Land.** The developer reports that the 60-hectare site is **already allocated to the project.** The exact legal instrument (freehold *titre foncier*, long lease *bail emphytéotique*, or a cooperative/state allocation) is to be confirmed, as it determines the mortgageability and security of the rental portfolio for a foreign investor (Appendix A; see also the risk analysis in Section 7).

## 4. Market — Social & Affordable Rental Housing

Senegal faces a structural housing deficit and rapid, partly-informal urbanization concentrated in the greater-Dakar corridor. Coeur-Moussa is explicitly positioned to reduce that deficit and curb informal settlement, in line with the national housing strategy — a positioning that supports both political goodwill and eligibility for affordable-housing incentives.

Crucially, this is a **rental** asset, not a build-to-sell development. The 2,000 villas are leased to members of the **Chantifa cooperative** under a single-owner structure, so the demand metric is **occupancy and rent collection**, not sales absorption. A committed cooperative tenant base materially de-risks demand relative to open-market sale, while defining the asset as a recurring-income (yield) investment. Rent levels, take-up commitments, and any government social-housing support are to be provided (Appendix A), together with comparable rent benchmarks from the greater-Dakar corridor.

## 5. Senegal — Country & Investment Climate

*Full detail and sources: `research/senegal_fdi_risk_compliance.md`. Figures should be re-verified with local counsel before any binding commitment.*

**Political.** Senegal is an electorally legitimate, constitutionally functioning democracy, long regarded as a West African "democratic beacon." However, as of mid-2026 it is in an acute, unresolved intra-government power struggle: in May 2026 President Bassirou Diomaye Faye dismissed Prime Minister Ousmane Sonko amid a dispute over debt-crisis strategy; Sonko was then elected Speaker of the National Assembly — a constitutionally protected office — and confirmed as leader of the ruling PASTEF party, giving him an independent power base. This is the single most time-sensitive factor in the country picture and is treated candidly in the risk analysis (Section 7).

**Macro & sovereign credit.** A 2024 debt-transparency audit found Senegal's real 2023 debt materially higher than previously reported (roughly 99.7% of GDP versus ~74.4% officially stated), triggering a suspended IMF program and one of the fastest sovereign-rating deteriorations of any country in the period — Moody's from Ba3 to Caa1 and S&P from B+ to CCC+ within roughly 14 months.

**A countervailing signal.** On governance quality the trend runs the other way: Senegal scored 46/100 on Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index (above the sub-Saharan average), entered the World Bank's B-READY top-10 in Africa for the first time in 2025, and was named among the top global improvers in the 2025 WJP Rule of Law Index. Governance quality and macro-fiscal stability are, unusually, moving in opposite directions — a nuance investors should hold in mind.

**Currency.** The West African CFA franc (XOF) is pegged to the euro at 655.957:1 under an unconditional French Treasury convertibility guarantee. Peg risk is low; the real currency consideration is administrative/liquidity friction in repatriating profits, given a 2025 tightening of BCEAO currency-surrender rules (Section 6).

**China-Senegal relations.** The relationship was upgraded to a "Comprehensive Strategic Cooperative Partnership" in September 2024. A June 2025 economic forum in Hangzhou produced MOUs explicitly covering housing, with Chinese participants flagging interest in low-cost social housing. China is already Senegal's largest source of imports. Recent flagship Chinese projects — the CNTIC/Huawei 2×50 MW solar-plus-storage program (2025), CGN Europe Energy's stake in the Malicounda solar plant, CRBC road construction, and Huawei digital infrastructure — provide strong credibility comparables (Sections 9-10).

## 6. Legal & Regulatory Compliance

*Full detail and sources: `research/senegal_fdi_risk_compliance.md`.*

**Investment Code.** Senegal adopted a new Investment Code in 2025 (Loi n°2025-16), offering a digitalized one-stop shop (10-working-day target), VAT suspension and customs-duty exemption on approved-project equipment during construction, and a fiscal-stability guarantee of 3 years (Dakar/Thiès) or 5 years (other regions). Approvals run through **APIX**, the investment promotion agency. Eligibility thresholds under the new Code should be confirmed with counsel.

**Foreign ownership & land tenure.** There is no nationality bar on holding titled Senegalese real estate; the binding constraint is land *category*. Senegal recognizes three tenure types in ascending order of security: *domaine national* (inalienable, highest risk), *domaine de l'État* (enabling a mortgageable, transferable long lease — *bail emphytéotique*), and *titre foncier* (full freehold, lowest risk once obtained). For a rental portfolio held by a project SPV and potentially pledged for financing, the instrument covering the allocated 60 hectares should be confirmed as secure and mortgageable (Section 7).

**Corporate vehicle.** The likely vehicle is an OHADA-form company (an SA for a multi-investor structure). Full foreign ownership is permitted with no local-partner requirement.

**Tax & incentives.** Corporate income tax is 30% and VAT 18%, but the Investment Code's construction-phase VAT suspension and customs exemptions, together with affordable-housing incentives tied to the national housing strategy and a specific renewable-energy VAT exemption (2020 decree), are directly relevant. A China-Senegal double-taxation treaty was signed in October 2023 but its entry into force is unconfirmed and should not be relied upon as currently operative.

**Repatriation.** Profit and capital repatriation is guaranteed in principle under the Investment Code but is procedural: it requires an FDI declaration, routing through an approved local bank, and compliance with BCEAO currency-surrender rules tightened in 2025. This is best framed to investors as a manageable compliance process, not a legal restriction.

**Dispute resolution.** Senegal belongs to OHADA (with the CCJA as both a supranational commercial court and an arbitration institution), the ICSID Convention, and the New York Convention. A workable pattern is CCJA arbitration for ordinary commercial/JV disputes, ICC/Paris arbitration for higher-value finance disputes, and ICSID/treaty arbitration reserved for sovereign-conduct claims — subject to confirming China-Senegal treaty coverage.

**Construction & environmental.** A *permis de construire* is required (via the digitalized TeleDAC platform; budget ~3 months in practice), together with an environmental and social impact assessment (EIES) via the DEEC. The applicable thresholds under the 2023 Environmental Code should be confirmed for a residential program of this scale.

## 7. Risk Assessment — Comprehensive ("Potential Crisis") Analysis

This section states each material risk plainly, with a concrete mitigation. It is the credibility anchor of the proposal and is deliberately not softened.

| Risk | Likelihood | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Faye-Sonko institutional rupture (May-June 2026)** — a live, unresolved power struggle with street-mobilization capacity | High (ongoing) | High | Monitor continuously through to signing; build a political-risk contingency and timing clause into the deal; do not treat as background |
| Sovereign debt / rating crisis (Caa1/CCC+, suspended IMF program) | High (ongoing) | Medium-High | Structure financing to avoid direct sovereign-credit exposure; price political-risk insurance into returns |
| Land tenure security (land reported already allocated; instrument unconfirmed) | Medium (partly de-risked) | High | Confirm the instrument (titre foncier / bail) is mortgageable and held by the rental SPV; full title-history search (état hypothécaire) via a notary before closing |
| Currency convertibility / repatriation friction (2025 BCEAO tightening) | Medium | Medium | Route all flows through an approved local bank from day one; retain UEMOA-qualified counsel; consider EUR-denominated tranches |
| CFA-exit / currency-reform political risk | Low-Medium (medium-term) | Medium | Disclose candidly; note Senegal's continued 2025 XOF bond issuance as a countervailing signal against near-term rupture |
| Regulatory / permitting delay | Medium | Medium | Budget ~3 months for permitting (not the optimistic 28-40 day target) in the construction schedule |
| Investor-state arbitration exposure (Senegal is an active ICSID respondent) | Low-Medium | Medium | Confirm China-Senegal BIT status before assuming treaty protection; structure via a jurisdiction with confirmed Senegal coverage if treaty protection is a priority |
| Expropriation risk | Low | High | MIGA / Sinosure political-risk insurance; the Investment Code's own expropriation guarantees |
| FX / PPA mismatch on the solar component | Medium (scenario-dependent) | Medium | Structure the PV offtake in EUR or EUR-indexed terms rather than USD (Section 9) |

**Recommended risk-transfer wrapper.** Because the Chinese participation is export-led, **Sinosure export-credit insurance** and, where relevant, **MIGA** political-risk cover are natural instruments to wrap the equipment/material exports and the equity exposure — turning several of the above risks into insurable, priced items rather than open exposures.

## 9. Solar PV Component — Multiple Scenario Options

*Full detail, cost sourcing, and citations: `research/senegal_solar_pv_options.md`.*

Because the base design contains no renewable element, the PV component is a clean, additive opportunity — and it is also a further line of Chinese equipment sold into the project (Section 10). Per client instruction, **multiple scenario options are presented rather than a single locked-in design.**

**Why the project structure is near-ideal for PV.** Coeur-Moussa is 2,000 villas on a single 60-hectare site, all held by **one owner as rental housing**. This means (i) a single landlord owns all 2,000 roofs, so there is no per-owner negotiation and the rooftop-rights uncertainty that affects condominium buildings is entirely moot — the landlord captures the electricity value directly; (ii) that same single, rent-earning owner is a far more bankable power-purchase counterparty than 2,000 fragmented homeowners would be; and (iii) the large contiguous site also supports a centralized array. Every scenario below is improved by the single-owner rental structure.

**Regulatory sweet spot (Decree 2023-286).** Solar PV of ≤1 MW per installation qualifies for a light "declaration" regime with no license; anything larger, or any installation exporting surplus to SENELEC, requires a 25-year CRSE license. Per-villa rooftop systems sit comfortably under the declaration threshold; a centralized 60-ha array would need the license.

| Scenario | Est. CAPEX | Ownership / role | Key consideration | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Rooftop distributed, per villa** | ~$1,000-1,400/kW (est.) | Single landlord SPV owns all arrays; Chinese partner supplies + installs (± O&M) | Dust-cleaning across 2,000 roofs favors a bundled Chinese O&M offer; rooftop-rights fully moot | Strong fit — each ≤1 MW system stays in the declaration regime; electricity value becomes an operating-cost saving or tenant-resale line |
| **(b) Centralized solar farm + private microgrid** | ~$1,000-1,300/kW (est.) | Landlord SPV owns; Chinese partner as EPC + possible minority equity | Triggers the 25-year CRSE license; uses part of the 60 ha | A "campus utility" feeding the whole town via a private microgrid |
| **(c) Hybrid PV + battery storage (± genset)** | PV + battery ~$250-450/kWh (est.) | Landlord SPV owns; Chinese partner supplies integrated PV + storage | Battery capex and ~10-15-year replacement cycle | **Recommended default** — best matched to Senegal's grid-reliability profile for a reliability-marketed rental product |
| **(d) PV + Chinese EPC / PPA or BOOT-BOO** | Borne by the Chinese side, recovered via a 15-25-year PPA | Chinese partner designs, finances, builds, owns, operates; SPV pays per-kWh | Counterparty credit materially improved (one rent-earning SPV, not 2,000 owners); USD/EUR-vs-XOF mismatch | Best if the Chinese side wants a long-term revenue stream and the SPV offers credit enhancement |

**Credibility anchor.** CGN Europe Energy — the European renewables arm of China General Nuclear Power Corporation — owns 90% of the operating 22 MW Malicounda solar plant in Senegal. This is the strongest direct Chinese-Senegal solar precedent and should lead the section. (No large Chinese-financed *utility-scale* Senegalese solar EPC project exists beyond this stake, so the project is best framed as extending that relationship into a new EPC/BOOT role.)

**Financing and de-risking.** Sinosure export-credit insurance, China Eximbank concessional lending (its Gouina hydro loan to Senegal ran 20-year/9-year-grace/2%), IFC and MIGA blended finance (the Kael+Kahone plants carried MIGA political-risk cover), and the AfDB "Desert to Power" facility all provide relevant support. **Currency:** because the XOF is pegged to the EUR, a EUR-denominated or EUR-indexed PPA carries materially lower currency risk than a USD-denominated one.

**Engineering note.** Dust and Harmattan soiling can cut output by 25-50% or more without active cleaning; roughly bi-weekly cleaning is recommended in the peak dry season. This should be budgeted into O&M for any chosen scenario — and an automated/robotic cleaning offer could be a differentiator in the Chinese partner's O&M package.

## 10. Strategic Rationale — A Market-Entry Play, Not Just a Financial Return

The core proposition to a Chinese partner is this: **Coeur-Moussa is a beachhead into the West African market for Chinese building materials, equipment, and companies.** Rental yield is the financial return; trade and market access is the strategic return.

**The building-materials export channel is concrete, not hypothetical.** SIVEP's own technical specifications already require imported steel rebar (FE500), aluminum joinery, ceramic and grès tiles, faience, sanitaryware, PVC piping, and electrical equipment — every one a category in which Chinese manufacturers are highly competitive. A program of 2,000 homes plus full town infrastructure is a large, repeatable materials order that a Chinese supplier base can fill, using Coeur-Moussa as a reference and demonstration project for the broader Senegalese and West African market. The solar component is part of the same export story.

This aligns with the upgraded 2024 China-Senegal Comprehensive Strategic Cooperative Partnership, the June 2025 Hangzhou forum's explicit interest in low-cost social housing, and China's position as Senegal's largest import supplier. Because the thesis is export-led, **Sinosure and China Eximbank are a natural fit** — they exist precisely to finance and de-risk Chinese equipment and material exports. Recent Chinese projects in Senegal (CNTIC/Huawei solar-plus-storage, CGN's Malicounda solar stake, CRBC roads, Huawei digital infrastructure) demonstrate that large Chinese-built projects are well received.

## 13. Exit Strategy

As a **hold-for-yield rental asset**, the base case is holding for recurring rental income rather than a near-term sale. Potential exit routes include sale of the stabilized income-producing portfolio to an institutional or regional buyer, refinance-and-hold, or a REIT/securitization vehicle. Separately, the Chinese materials-supply relationship established through this project may carry ongoing value and continuity independent of any equity exit — a strategic asset in its own right.
